LocalVoice vs Dessa

Block's Dessa is the most human-sounding voice AI shown in public so far. We measured all 21 of its public demo conversations, then built a voice that runs entirely on a laptop with a 4 GB graphics card and measured it the same way.

What Dessa's demos give away

Block's page serves each demo as two lossless tracks, one per speaker, with timed subtitles. Measuring them directly shows how the system is built, even though Block has published no paper or model card.

Other measurements: the demo machine's speaker echo shows up in some tracks 70–140 ms late, which puts the setup's audio round trip at about 0.1 s. No fixed-rate artefact survives in Dessa's output, so its internal frame rate is not visible in these files.

How fast each one answers

Time from the moment the other side stops talking to the moment this side starts. Negative means it started before the other finished. LocalVoice was measured in real-time conversations with a simulated person who waits, pauses mid-thought, says "mm-hm", and sometimes interrupts, using human response times taken from the Dessa recordings.

What was built

Two models, one voice. The speech model transcribes the person live while they talk, so their words are ready about 0.07 s after they stop. A small text model writes the reply as Juno, and every sentence is checked before any sound is made: help-desk lines and repeats are dropped, and it may not tack a question onto every turn. The speech model then speaks it in its built-in voice, the same one it uses when it chats by itself. It needs care to be fast on one 4 GB card: speech starts about 0.1 s after it is asked, but 0.35 s if the text model is still writing on the same card, so the first sentence is written, its sound is started, and only then is the rest written.

The timing rules are calibrated, not guessed. The "is the person done?" thresholds were tuned on 118 real turn ends and 342 mid-sentence pauses from Dessa's subtitled demos. The transcript adds a second opinion: words ending mid-sentence ("...and", no full stop) make the voice wait longer, and a question mark makes it answer sooner. The reply is written and voiced, unheard, during the pause, but the sound is held until the pause reaches 0.3–0.4 s, so a person who was only pausing rarely hears it jump in. A reply that opens with "Oh," or "Yeah," says that word from ready-made clips the moment it is written, the way Dessa opens 44% of its turns and people 40%. When talked over it softens at 0.25 s and stops at 0.65 s, because 88% of people's "mm-hm"s in the demos end before then; but if both started at once, it backs off after 0.25 s, a second burst of speech soon after the first also makes it stop, and once the person has cut in it lets them finish before speaking again. It also adapts: each audible early start makes it a little more patient with that person.

Without headphones, the laptop speakers leak into the microphone. An echo canceller and an echo gate keep the voice from hearing itself. In two simulated speaker conversations it stopped itself 5 times while the person interrupted 6 times; before the gate it stopped itself 53 times. It still misses some interruptions over speakers, so headphones are better.

What three live test calls showed

A person talked to LocalVoice three times through a small call window, over the laptop speakers, and said out loud whatever felt off. Both sides were recorded on the laptop, transcribed, and lined up with what the voice was doing at each moment.

Can Dessa itself be rebuilt here?

Not by training from scratch on this laptop. Using the standard estimate (compute ≈ 6 × parameters × audio frames, at 12.5 frames per second):

/*MATH_ROWS*/
ModelAudioThis laptopOne rented H100Rental cost

Compute is not the real barrier: a Dessa-scale run costs about a thousand dollars of rented time. Data is. Dessa learned from real two-person conversations at a scale (roughly a million hours, by our estimate) that no open dataset offers. A small proof-of-concept model could train on this laptop in hours, but published models of that size babble.

Next steps

Test the split design live Recommended next

The benchmark is a simulated person. A few calls with the call window will show how the new design feels by ear. Each call is transcribed and lined up with what the voice did, so every awkward moment becomes a specific fix.

Close the timing gap with a predictive turn model

Dessa starts before you finish. Matching that needs a small model that predicts the end of your sentence from sound and words, trained on open conversation recordings. That is a few dollars of temporary cloud time and needs no personal data.

Teach the text model the style

Retraining the voice model is no longer needed. If the wording should get livelier, a small text-only fine-tune of the 1.2B text model would do it, and it fits on this laptop's card. Details in RETRAINING-PLAN.md.

A bigger graphics card

A 12–16 GB card would run today's 7B full-duplex models (Moshi, NVIDIA's PersonaPlex), which listen and talk at the same time like Dessa.

Method and sources

Audio measured with open signal processing (spectra, voice activity per track, pitch with Praat). Speech detection agrees with Block's subtitles on 71–94% of subtitled speech; the rest is mostly silence inside subtitle lines. Block chose these recordings. LocalVoice numbers come from a simulated person, not a real one.
